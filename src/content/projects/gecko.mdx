<p class="lede">Every alternative keyboard layout claims to be better than QWERTY, and they're mostly scored by the same handful of community analyzers using hand-picked weights. GECKO is my attempt to do it properly: score layouts with a cost model checked against how people actually type, search for the best one, and report how sure the answer is.</p>

## The answer, for English

Searching the 30 letter keys of a standard keyboard against a mix of web, chat and book English, every search converges on the same layout:

```
c w d l x   j y o u .
g s t n v   p h e i a
f b m r q   z k ' / ,
```

All 20 random seeds reach it, and so do 84 of 104 re-runs with perturbed weights (the median one moves just 2 keys). Branch-and-bound search proves no better arrangement of its 16 most common letters exists. It puts the consonants under the left hand and `h e i a` under the right, so most words alternate hands at the vowel. It rolls outward far more than inward and parks the rare letters in the hard-to-reach center columns.

## How a layout is scored

A score is a weighted sum of metrics over letter, pair and triple frequencies: same-finger bigrams, rolls, redirects, stretches, pinky–ring pairs, finger load, and more. 47 of them are implemented. Most layout analyzers stop there and pick weights by feel. GECKO fits its weights to evidence:

- **Typing time.** Key-pair intervals from the Aalto 136M-keystroke dataset (about 49,000 QWERTY typists). Same-finger pairs cost about 17% more time; switching hands saves 8%.
- **Comfort.** The Engram study's measured preferences (61 people) enter as their own term.
- **Held-out judges.** The weights come from a bilevel search scored on timing data from typists it never trained on. A score that agrees with itself proves nothing.

For physical keyboards there's also a hand model. A skeleton hand is placed on the board, and inverse kinematics solves each finger reaching each key. That gives per-key reach, press angle and wrist cost, so ortholinear, column-staggered and 3D curved boards are scored by the hand that types on them, not by a flat grid.

## How it searches

The core is Rust, written for speed. Swapping two keys rescores only the pairs and triples those keys touch, so a single run can test tens of millions of layouts in a couple of minutes. The optimizers share that evaluator:

- **Simulated annealing, tabu search, breakout local search**, and a **memetic** (genetic plus local search) hybrid for single answers.
- **MAP-Elites** to map the space of good layouts by style (roll-heavy, alternation-heavy, …), not just one optimum.
- **NSGA-II** for Pareto fronts when two goals pull against each other.
- **Branch and bound** to certify a result exactly over the most frequent letters.

Every run records its seed, corpus, profile and commit, and `gecko verify` replays it bit-for-bit. The web UI compiles the same scorer to WebAssembly, so the browser and the CLI never disagree.

## Beyond letters

Small split keyboards put symbols and numbers on layers. Nobody had measured what a layer press costs, so I used the nearest proxy, a held Shift, across 56,000 typists. It makes the next key 1.78× slower, about as bad as 3.7 same-finger pairs. Charging that real cost changes the answer: keep `' = , .` on the base, put every other symbol and digit on one layer, and put Esc on a thumb if you use Vim. Keeping `h j k l` usable for Vim turns out to cost almost nothing.

The board itself is a variable too. Boards load from TOML, keyboard-layout-editor files, or parameters like stagger, splay, split and 3D keywell curvature, and the optimizer can shape the board alongside the layout.
